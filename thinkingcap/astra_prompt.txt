TASK. You are given the weights of a language model, Qwen3-0.6B (28 layers of width 1,024, all of them full-attention layers with 16 query heads and 8 key/value heads of size 128; each layer has a SwiGLU MLP of width 3,072; RMSNorm; vocabulary 151,936; input embedding and output layer tied). It is a reasoning model: it thinks between <think> and </think>, then answers. Your job is to make it THINK SHORTER WITHOUT LOSING ACCURACY by EDITING ITS WEIGHTS DIRECTLY: find out where the model wastes thinking tokens, identify the components responsible, and repair that with targeted changes.

HOW IT WILL BE MEASURED. After you finish, your edited model and the unedited model answer the same held-out test questions of four kinds, with this fixed protocol: the chat template with thinking enabled; sampling with temperature 0.6, top_p 0.95, top_k 20; at most 8,192 new tokens for code questions and 4,096 for the others (reasoning and answer together; an answer cut at the limit counts as wrong). The kinds: math word problems (type "svamp": the final number is graded), Python functions (type "mbpp": hidden unit tests), instruction following (type "ifeval": checked constraints such as length, format, keywords), and function calling (types "bfcl_simple_python", "bfcl_multiple": pick the right function with the right arguments, and "bfcl_irrelevance": call no function when none fits). The score is the number of thinking tokens saved, and the accuracy must not drop by more than about one point; fewer tokens at the same accuracy is the goal. The config, tokenizer, chat template and decoding settings are fixed: only weight values can change (same tensor names and shapes). You never see the test questions.

WHAT COUNTS. We want edits that come from understanding the network: find out what makes it think longer than it needs, identify the components responsible (specific neurons, heads, directions or circuits) and change them in a way you can justify with evidence. Repairing a specific failure mechanism is in scope: for example, if the model sometimes repeats itself until it is cut off, finding and fixing what drives that repetition counts, and so does removing a specific wasteful habit inside the thinking. Out of scope, and refused by save_edit: the final norm (model.norm.weight), the input embedding / output layer (model.embed_tokens.weight, lm_head.weight), and any edit that only multiplies a whole tensor by a constant. Global knobs (making the whole output distribution sharper or flatter) are not what we are looking for, and neither is making the model skip its thinking or close it early regardless of the problem: the thinking it needs must stay.

HOW TO WORK.
1. Diagnose first. Look at where the model spends its thinking on practice problems: which types think longest, which answers are cut at the limit (often while repeating themselves), how much of a right answer comes after the answer is already settled. The generation summary counts these; read some long texts. Go after the largest waste you can explain.
2. Localize causally. That a component is more active in long answers is not enough (long answers come from harder problems). Before relying on a component, show that changing it changes the behaviour you predicted, and try several strengths to see a dose-response.
3. Measure with enough power. Accuracy over n generations has a standard error of about 50/sqrt(n) percentage points: about 4.4 points at 128 generations, 2.2 at 512. Compare original and edited weights on the SAME problems: a generation pass with "model": "both" does this with the same random seeds and reports the paired difference with its standard error; do not compare with a measurement on other problems. Keep an edit only if it saves thinking tokens clearly on problems that were not used to choose it and its accuracy difference is no worse than about one point beyond its standard error. Fewer thinking tokens bought with lost accuracy is a failure. Dense measurements help too and are cheap: one teacher-forced pass can read many of the model's own long versus short answers and gives a signal on every token. Edits that change a tensor by a fraction of a percent usually have effects too small to see; test strengths large enough to matter and back off if they hurt.
4. Plan your forward passes (you have only {PASSES}): for example one diagnostic generation, teacher-forced passes for dense paired measurements, and a final paired generation ("model": "both") on problems you have not used to confirm.
5. Work efficiently: state the hypothesis of each code block in its first comment line; work through the model in parts; load things once into kernel variables; print compact summaries, never whole arrays (and never raw lists of vocabulary tokens; print token ids or short excerpts of generated texts, at most about 300 characters each); keep {WORK}/notes.md up to date (findings with numbers, edits tried and their measured effect, the current best edit; it is shown to you again if the conversation is restarted); keep copies of good edits.
