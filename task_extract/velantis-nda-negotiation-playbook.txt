This Playbook governs the negotiation of non-disclosure agreements in connection with mergers, acquisitions, divestitures, joint ventures, and other strategic transactions initiated or evaluated by Velantis Technologies, Inc. ("Velantis"). All members of the Legal Department, and any outside counsel engaged to negotiate NDAs on Velantis's behalf, must follow the positions set forth below. Deviations from a "Hard-Line" position require written approval from the VP & Associate General Counsel or the General Counsel. No attorney — whether in-house or outside counsel — is authorized to bind Velantis to any position inconsistent with this Playbook absent the express written approval described herein. This Playbook supersedes all prior internal guidance memoranda, email instructions, and informal advice regarding NDA negotiation in the M&A context.

Section 1 — How to Use This Playbook
1.1 — Structure
For each substantive NDA provision addressed in this Playbook, four tiers of guidance are provided. These tiers are designed to give negotiating attorneys clear authority and to ensure consistency across the Legal Department's M&A practice. The four tiers are as follows:
(a) Preferred Position. This tier reflects Velantis's standard language as set forth in the v.6.2 form of Mutual Non-Disclosure Agreement (the "v.6.2 form"). The Preferred Position is the opening position in every negotiation and should be maintained wherever possible. The assigned attorney should present the Preferred Position as Velantis's starting point and should not offer concessions proactively. Concessions should only be made in response to a specific counterparty request and only to the extent authorized by this Playbook.
(b) Acceptable Without Escalation. This tier identifies modifications or counterparty positions that the assigned attorney may accept without further internal approval, provided all other Playbook requirements are satisfied. The purpose of this tier is to empower negotiating attorneys to move efficiently through NDA discussions without unnecessary internal process, while maintaining appropriate guardrails. An attorney who accepts a position within this tier should document the modification in the negotiation log and note that it falls within Playbook authority.
(c) Requires Escalation. This tier describes counterparty positions that may be commercially reasonable in certain circumstances but that require approval from Rachel Sung (VP & Associate General Counsel) or, in her absence, the General Counsel before acceptance. The escalation process is described in detail in Section 18 of this Playbook. The assigned attorney should not convey agreement — even tentatively or subject to confirmation — to a counterparty on any matter requiring escalation until internal approval has been received.
(d) Hard Line — Will Not Concede. This tier identifies positions that Velantis will not accept under any circumstances. Hard-Line items represent points on which the Legal Department has determined that no concession is warranted regardless of commercial context. If a counterparty insists on any Hard-Line item after good-faith negotiation, the matter must be escalated to the General Counsel for a determination on whether to terminate discussions. Hard-Line items are not subject to override by deal-team members, business-unit leaders, or outside counsel. Only the General Counsel (or, in extraordinary circumstances, the Chief Executive Officer acting on the General Counsel's recommendation) may authorize a deviation from a Hard-Line position.
1.2 — Scope of Application
This Playbook applies to all NDAs executed in connection with potential M&A transactions, whether Velantis is on the buy side or the sell side. The term "M&A transactions" is broadly defined for these purposes to include mergers, stock acquisitions, asset acquisitions, divestitures, spin-offs, carve-outs, joint ventures, strategic investments, and any other transaction in which Velantis may acquire or dispose of a business, business unit, product line, or material asset portfolio.
Where guidance differs depending on Velantis's posture in the transaction (i.e., buy side versus sell side), the relevant section will so note with a clear designation. Unless otherwise indicated, the guidance in this Playbook assumes Velantis is acting as the potential acquiror (buy side), as this is the most common posture in Velantis's current strategic pipeline. Attorneys negotiating sell-side NDAs should review each section for any buy-side/sell-side distinction and should consult Rachel Sung if the applicable guidance is ambiguous in the sell-side context.
This Playbook does not apply to NDAs entered into for purposes other than M&A transactions, including (without limitation) NDAs for vendor relationships, technology licensing discussions, research collaborations, or employment-related confidentiality agreements. Separate guidance exists for those categories and is maintained by the relevant practice groups within the Legal Department.
1.3 — Use of Outside Counsel
When outside counsel (currently Kirkner Pratt LLP, though other firms may be engaged from time to time) is conducting NDA negotiations on behalf of Velantis, the responsible partner at the outside firm must be provided a copy of this Playbook prior to the commencement of negotiations and must confirm in writing (email is sufficient) that such partner has reviewed the Playbook and will adhere to its terms. The confirmation should be directed to the in-house attorney supervising the engagement.
Outside counsel should not accept any position designated "Requires Escalation" or "Hard Line" without express Velantis in-house approval. For the avoidance of doubt, the escalation authority for outside counsel runs through the supervising in-house attorney and then to Rachel Sung or the General Counsel — outside counsel should not contact Rachel Sung or the General Counsel directly for escalation approvals unless the supervising in-house attorney is unavailable and the matter is time-sensitive.
Outside counsel should also be advised that Velantis's internal project code names are confidential and should not be disclosed to counterparties or their counsel during negotiations. The NDA itself should refer to the transaction in generic terms (e.g., "the potential transaction between the parties") unless the deal team instructs otherwise.
1.4 — Document Management
All NDA negotiations should be conducted in Microsoft Word using the Track Changes function, with each turn saved under Velantis's standard file-naming convention:
[Project Code Name] — NDA — v[X] — [Party Name] — [Date (YYYY-MM-DD)]
Example: Project Falcon — NDA — v3 — Counterparty LLC — 2024-11-15
The v.6.2 form is the base document for all outgoing first drafts. If a counterparty sends its own form NDA as the initial draft, the assigned attorney should prepare a redline against the v.6.2 form to identify deviations and should use this Playbook to evaluate each deviation. In most circumstances, Velantis's strong preference is to negotiate from the v.6.2 form rather than a counterparty's form; the assigned attorney should request that the counterparty accept the v.6.2 form as the base document before agreeing to negotiate from the counterparty's paper.
All drafts, redlines, and related correspondence should be saved to the matter folder on Velantis's document management system (currently NetDocuments) and tagged with the appropriate project code name and matter number.
Section 2 — Definition of Confidential Information
2.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form defines "Confidential Information" broadly to include all information furnished by or on behalf of the Disclosing Party (or any of its Representatives) to the Receiving Party (or any of its Representatives) in connection with the Permitted Purpose, whether oral, written, electronic, or visual, and regardless of the manner in which such information is furnished. The definition further encompasses all analyses, compilations, studies, notes, memoranda, or other documents or materials prepared by the Receiving Party or its Representatives that contain, reflect, or are derived from, in whole or in part, any such information (collectively, "Derivative Materials").
This broad, all-encompassing definition is intentional. It ensures that Velantis does not need to mark or designate information as "confidential" at the time of disclosure in order for the NDA's protections to apply. The definition also captures Derivative Materials, which is critical to ensuring that the Receiving Party cannot circumvent the NDA by extracting confidential data into its own analytical frameworks and then treating those frameworks as unprotected.
The assigned attorney should maintain this definition in its current form. Attempts to narrow the definition should be resisted unless they fall within the parameters set forth below.
2.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
The following counterparty proposals regarding the definition of Confidential Information are acceptable without escalation:
•  Illustrative Categories. A counterparty may propose adding specific illustrative categories as examples of Confidential Information. Common examples include financial projections, business plans, customer and supplier lists, pricing information, source code, algorithms, employee data, technical specifications, and strategic plans. Such additions are acceptable and often helpful for clarity, as they signal to business personnel reviewing the NDA what types of information are covered. The assigned attorney should confirm, however, that the illustrative categories are presented as non-exclusive examples (e.g., "including, without limitation" or "including, but not limited to") and do not purport to limit the general definition. A formulation such as "'Confidential Information' means the following categories of information: [list]" is not acceptable, as it converts the definition from a broad catch-all into a closed list.
•  Oral Disclosure Confirmation. A counterparty may propose that oral disclosures be identified as confidential at the time of disclosure and confirmed in writing within a specified period. This is a common request, particularly from counterparties with established information-governance programs. A ten (10) business-day written-confirmation requirement for oral disclosures is acceptable without escalation. The assigned attorney should ensure that the confirmation requirement applies only to oral disclosures and does not extend to written, electronic, or visual disclosures, which should be covered automatically under the broad definition. Written-confirmation periods shorter than ten (10) business days (e.g., five (5) business days or three (3) business days) require escalation, as they may impose impractical burdens on Velantis's deal team, particularly during intensive due-diligence sessions involving numerous oral discussions.
2.3 — Exceptions to Confidential Information
The definition of Confidential Information is subject to customary exceptions. The treatment of these exceptions is addressed below.
2.3(a) — Preferred Position (Standard Exceptions).  The v.6.2 form contains four standard exceptions to the definition of Confidential Information. Information that falls within any of these exceptions is not treated as Confidential Information for purposes of the agreement. The four exceptions are:
(i) information that is or becomes publicly available or part of the public domain through no fault of, and without any action or inaction by, the Receiving Party or any of its Representatives;
(ii) information that is or was already in the Receiving Party's possession prior to disclosure by or on behalf of the Disclosing Party, as demonstrated by competent documentary evidence;
(iii) information that is received by the Receiving Party from a third party that, to the Receiving Party's knowledge after reasonable inquiry, is not under a confidentiality obligation to the Disclosing Party with respect to such information; and
(iv) information that is independently developed by the Receiving Party or its Representatives without use of or reference to any Confidential Information, as demonstrated by competent documentary evidence.
These exceptions are market-standard and should be maintained in substantially this form in every NDA. Each exception serves a critical function: exception (i) recognizes that information in the public domain cannot meaningfully be kept secret; exception (ii) protects information the Receiving Party already possessed through its own efforts; exception (iii) permits the Receiving Party to receive information from independent third-party sources; and exception (iv) preserves the Receiving Party's ability to develop its own intellectual property and business knowledge independently.
The assigned attorney should pay particular attention to the burden of proof embedded in exceptions (ii) and (iv) — both require "competent documentary evidence," which sets a meaningful evidentiary threshold. This language should be retained.
2.3(b) — Acceptable Without Escalation.  The following modifications to the standard exceptions are acceptable without escalation:
•  Independent-Development Exception — "Without Reference To" Formulation. A counterparty may propose modifying the independent-development exception to read "without reference to" Confidential Information rather than "without use of or reference to" Confidential Information. The formulation "without reference to" is substantively equivalent because "reference to" inherently encompasses "use of" in this context — one cannot reference information without, in some sense, using it. This modification is acceptable without concern.
•  Public-Availability Exception — Minor Wording Variations. Minor wording variations to the public-availability exception, such as "generally available to the public" in place of "publicly available" or "part of the public domain," are acceptable provided the critical qualifier — "through no fault of the Receiving Party" (or substantially equivalent language, such as "through no act or omission of the Receiving Party") — is retained. The assigned attorney should focus on preserving the qualifier rather than insisting on any particular formulation of the concept of public availability.
2.3(c) — Requires Escalation.  The following proposals regarding exceptions to Confidential Information require escalation to Rachel Sung or the General Counsel before acceptance:
•  Industry-Availability Exception. Proposals to add an exception for information that becomes "generally available within" a particular industry (e.g., "the data analytics industry," "the semiconductor industry," "the enterprise software industry") must be escalated. Such carve-outs are overbroad and risk swallowing the definition of Confidential Information, particularly where Velantis and the counterparty operate in the same or adjacent industries. Information may be "generally available" within an industry — for example, a particular pricing model or technical architecture — without being available to the public at large. The proper protection for the Receiving Party is the public-availability exception (clause (i) above), which requires that the information be available to the public at large, not merely known within a specialized sector. In most cases, an industry-availability exception should be rejected. However, because there may be narrow circumstances in which such an exception is appropriate (e.g., where the parties are in unrelated industries), escalation is required rather than a blanket Hard-Line prohibition.
2.3(d) — Hard Line.  
•  "Through No Fault" Qualifier. Velantis will not accept any formulation that removes or dilutes the "through no fault of the Receiving Party" qualifier (or its substantive equivalent) from the public-availability exception. This qualifier is essential to prevent a party from engineering a disclosure — whether directly or through a third-party intermediary — and then claiming that the information has entered the public domain. Without this qualifier, a Receiving Party could leak Confidential Information, wait for it to circulate publicly, and then assert that the public-availability exception applies. This outcome is fundamentally inconsistent with the purpose of the NDA, and Velantis will not accept it. If a counterparty proposes removing or weakening this qualifier, the assigned attorney must reject the proposal and, if the counterparty persists, escalate to the General Counsel for a determination on whether to terminate discussions.
Section 3 — Definition of Representatives
3.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form defines "Representatives" to mean the Receiving Party's directors, officers, employees, attorneys (including in-house counsel), accountants, financial advisors, and other agents or consultants who have a need to know the Confidential Information for the Permitted Purpose. The "need to know" requirement is an important limiter: it prevents a Receiving Party from distributing Confidential Information broadly within its organization and instead confines access to those individuals who genuinely require the information in order to evaluate the contemplated transaction.
The assigned attorney should ensure that the "need to know" qualifier is retained in every negotiation. This qualifier operates as a complement to the standard-of-care provisions (see Section 7) and helps to establish a framework of responsible information handling.
3.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
The following modifications to the definition of Representatives are acceptable without escalation, subject to the conditions described below:
•  Inclusion of Affiliates. A counterparty may propose expanding the definition of Representatives to include "affiliates and their respective directors, officers, employees, attorneys, accountants, financial advisors, and other agents or consultants." The inclusion of affiliates and their personnel is a common request, particularly from counterparties that are part of a larger corporate group or private equity portfolio. This modification is acceptable provided the counterparty also accepts language making the Receiving Party responsible for any breach of the agreement by its Representatives, including any affiliate or affiliate personnel. Specifically, the breach-responsibility provision must state that the Receiving Party shall be liable for any breach of the NDA's terms by any of its Representatives as if such breach had been committed by the Receiving Party itself. This language ensures that the Disclosing Party has a single point of accountability and is not required to pursue individual affiliates (which may be organized in foreign jurisdictions or have limited assets) in the event of a breach.
•  Conditional Acceptance. If the counterparty proposes adding affiliates to the definition of Representatives but resists the inclusion of breach-responsibility language binding the Receiving Party for its affiliates' actions, the matter must be escalated to Rachel Sung. In the absence of breach-responsibility language, the inclusion of affiliates is not acceptable, as it effectively broadens the universe of permitted recipients without providing Velantis any recourse against the Receiving Party if an affiliate misuses Confidential Information.
3.3 — Hard Line
•  Affiliates Without Breach Responsibility. Velantis will not accept a Representatives definition that includes affiliates without corresponding breach-responsibility language binding the Receiving Party for its affiliates' actions. This is a non-negotiable point. The rationale is straightforward: permitting a Receiving Party to share Confidential Information with affiliates while disclaiming responsibility for those affiliates' conduct creates an unacceptable enforcement gap. Velantis's position is that the party to the NDA must stand behind the conduct of every individual and entity to whom it grants access to Confidential Information.
Section 4 — Permitted Purpose
4.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form defines "Permitted Purpose" as "evaluating a potential strategic transaction between the parties." This language is intentionally broad to accommodate a range of deal structures — including mergers, stock purchases, asset purchases, joint ventures, and minority investments — without requiring the parties to commit to a specific transaction form at the NDA stage. The broad formulation also avoids the need to amend the NDA if the parties' discussions evolve from one transaction structure to another during the diligence process.
4.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
•  Narrowing to Specific Transaction. A counterparty may propose narrowing the Permitted Purpose to describe the specific transaction under consideration. Common formulations include "evaluating a potential acquisition by Velantis of [Target Company]," "evaluating a potential acquisition by Velantis of the [identified business unit or division] of [Target Company]," or "evaluating a potential merger of equals between Velantis and [Target Company]." Narrowing the Permitted Purpose to match the actual transaction scope is acceptable and may, depending on the circumstances, be affirmatively desirable. A narrow Permitted Purpose limits the Receiving Party's right to use the Confidential Information, which can provide important protections when Velantis is on the sell side.
Before accepting a narrowed Permitted Purpose, the assigned attorney should confirm with the deal team that the proposed narrowed language accurately describes the contemplated transaction and does not inadvertently exclude potential transaction structures that are under active consideration. For example, if the deal team is considering both an acquisition of the target's equity and an acquisition of a specific asset portfolio, the Permitted Purpose should be drafted broadly enough to encompass both structures.
4.3 — Requires Escalation
The following proposals regarding the Permitted Purpose require escalation:
•  Broadening Beyond the Transaction. Proposals to broaden the Permitted Purpose beyond the scope of the actual transaction under consideration require escalation. Examples of overbroad Permitted Purpose language include "evaluating a potential transaction or any future business relationship between the parties," "evaluating any commercial arrangement between the parties," or "exploring strategic opportunities, including but not limited to a potential transaction." Such formulations effectively convert the NDA into a general business-relationship NDA, which is governed by separate Velantis policies and forms. The assigned attorney should push back on such proposals and explain that the NDA is intended to cover only the specific M&A transaction under discussion. If the counterparty insists, the matter should be escalated to Rachel Sung for a determination on whether the broader language is appropriate in the particular commercial context.
Section 5 — Confidentiality Term
5.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form provides that the confidentiality obligations imposed by the agreement shall survive for a period of two (2) years from the date of the agreement. This two-year term is market-standard for M&A NDAs and reflects the Legal Department's judgment that two years is an appropriate period during which pre-signing diligence information retains meaningful competitive sensitivity. After two years, the vast majority of financial projections, pipeline data, and strategic plans shared during M&A diligence will have been overtaken by subsequent developments and will no longer pose a material competitive risk if disclosed.
The two-year term also reflects practical enforceability considerations. An NDA with a very long or indefinite confidentiality period is more difficult to enforce, as courts may view such terms as unreasonable restraints, and as a practical matter, it becomes increasingly difficult over time to demonstrate that information remains confidential and that a particular disclosure caused actionable harm.
5.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
•  Shorter Terms. Terms shorter than two years are acceptable without escalation if the deal team confirms that the expected shelf life of the Confidential Information supports a shorter period. For example, a deal involving rapidly evolving technology or time-sensitive market data may warrant a shorter confidentiality term (e.g., eighteen (18) months or one (1) year). The assigned attorney should obtain written confirmation (email is sufficient) from the deal-team lead that a shorter term is acceptable from a business perspective before agreeing to the modification.
•  No Longer-Than-Two-Year Terms Within This Tier. For the avoidance of doubt, no modification within this tier permits a confidentiality term longer than two (2) years. Any proposal to extend the term beyond two years is a Hard-Line item and must be handled in accordance with Section 5.4 below.
5.3 — Requires Escalation
•  None. Any proposal exceeding the two-year term is handled exclusively as a Hard-Line item under Section 5.4.
5.4 — Hard Line
•  Maximum Two-Year Term. The confidentiality term must not exceed two (2) years from the date of the agreement. Proposals for longer terms — including three (3) years, five (5) years, and perpetual or indefinite terms — are rejected. If a counterparty insists on a longer term, the assigned attorney must escalate to Rachel Sung or the General Counsel for a determination on how to proceed, including whether to terminate discussions if the counterparty is intransigent.
Rationale: A two-year term is market standard for M&A NDAs. A survey of NDAs filed as exhibits to Schedule 13D and Schedule TO filings with the Securities and Exchange Commission over the past three years confirms that the median confidentiality term for M&A NDAs is two years, with a range of one to three years. Longer terms impose disproportionate compliance burdens — including monitoring obligations, employee-training requirements, and litigation-hold considerations — that are not justified by the anticipated shelf life of pre-signing diligence information. Moreover, longer terms increase the likelihood of inadvertent breaches, which could expose Velantis to claims and litigation costs even in the absence of any actual harm to the Disclosing Party.
Section 6 — Residuals Clause
6.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form includes a residuals clause, and this clause is a must-have for Velantis in every M&A NDA. The residuals clause provides that nothing in the agreement shall restrict the Receiving Party's right to use "Residual Information" in the course of the Receiving Party's business. "Residual Information" is defined as ideas, concepts, know-how, techniques, methodologies, or other general knowledge retained in the unaided memories of the Receiving Party's Representatives who have had access to Confidential Information in connection with the Permitted Purpose.
The residuals clause recognizes a practical reality: when Velantis's employees participate in diligence processes and review a counterparty's business information, those employees will inevitably retain general impressions, concepts, and knowledge from that review. It would be impractical — and in many cases, impossible — to require employees to "unlearn" everything they absorbed during diligence. The residuals clause provides a safe harbor for this inevitable retention, provided the retained knowledge exists in the employee's unaided memory (i.e., the employee is not consulting notes, copies of documents, or other tangible records of the Confidential Information).
The residuals clause is critical to Velantis's business operations. Velantis regularly evaluates potential acquisitions in the data analytics, artificial intelligence, and cloud infrastructure sectors. Many of these evaluations do not result in a completed transaction. Without a residuals clause, Velantis's personnel could be exposed to claims that their subsequent work — including product development, investment decisions, and competitive strategy — was improperly influenced by Confidential Information received during a prior, unconsummated diligence process. The residuals clause eliminates this risk and allows Velantis to operate its business freely after a failed deal process.
6.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
The following modifications to the residuals clause are acceptable without escalation:
•  Trade-Secret Carve-Out. A counterparty may propose qualifying the residuals clause to exclude trade secrets, as defined under applicable law (e.g., the Defend Trade Secrets Act, 18 U.S.C. § 1836, or the Uniform Trade Secrets Act as adopted in the applicable jurisdiction). This qualification is acceptable. Trade secrets, by definition, derive their value from their secrecy and are subject to separate legal protections that exist independently of the NDA. Excluding trade secrets from the residuals clause does not materially impair Velantis's operational flexibility, as trade secrets represent a narrow subset of the information typically shared during M&A diligence.
•  "Highly Confidential — No Residuals" Marking. A counterparty may propose qualifying the residuals clause to exclude information that the Disclosing Party designates in writing as "Highly Confidential — No Residuals" at the time of disclosure. This qualification is acceptable and reflects a reasonable compromise: it allows the Disclosing Party to identify specific categories of particularly sensitive information that should not be subject to the residuals safe harbor, while preserving the residuals clause for the vast majority of Confidential Information exchanged during diligence. The assigned attorney should confirm that the marking requirement is clear and operational — the designation must be made in writing and at the time of disclosure, not retroactively.
•  Either or Both Qualifications. Either or both of the foregoing qualifications may be accepted without escalation. Together, they appropriately balance Velantis's operational flexibility with legitimate protections for a counterparty's most sensitive information.
•  No IP License Confirmation. A counterparty may propose adding language confirming that the residuals clause does not grant a license to any patent, copyright, trademark, or other intellectual property right of the Disclosing Party. This is acceptable and, in fact, is already implicit in the v.6.2 form's general "no rights" language. Making it explicit in the residuals clause does not change the legal effect and may provide useful clarity for both parties.
6.3 — Requires Escalation
The following proposals regarding the residuals clause require escalation:
•  Limitation to Specific Information Categories. Proposals to limit the residuals clause to specific categories of information (beyond the trade-secret and "Highly Confidential — No Residuals" carve-outs described above) require escalation. For example, a counterparty might propose that the residuals clause applies only to "general industry knowledge and practices" or only to "non-technical business information." Such limitations are disfavored because they introduce ambiguity about which types of retained knowledge are protected by the residuals clause and which are not, creating a fact-intensive dispute risk that the residuals clause is designed to avoid.
•  Time-Limited Residuals. Proposals to impose a time limit on the residuals clause (e.g., residuals rights terminate six (6) months after the return or destruction of materials) require escalation. The purpose of the residuals clause is to address information that persists in an individual's unaided memory. Memory does not operate on a defined schedule, and imposing a time limit on the residuals clause reintroduces the very risk — post-deal intellectual contamination claims — that the clause is designed to eliminate.
6.4 — Hard Line
•  Deletion of Residuals Clause. Velantis will not agree to an NDA that does not contain a residuals clause. Deletion of the residuals clause in its entirety is a deal-breaker. The residuals clause is classified as a "must-have" provision for Velantis, and its inclusion is non-negotiable. If a counterparty refuses to include any form of residuals protection — even with the trade-secret and "Highly Confidential — No Residuals" carve-outs offered as compromises — the matter must be escalated to the General Counsel immediately for a determination on whether to terminate discussions. In the Legal Department's experience, the absence of a residuals clause exposes Velantis to an unacceptable level of litigation risk, particularly given the frequency with which Velantis evaluates (but does not consummate) potential acquisitions in overlapping technology sectors.
Section 7 — Standard of Care
7.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form requires the Receiving Party to protect Confidential Information using "reasonable care, and in no event less than the same degree of care the Receiving Party uses to protect its own confidential information of a similar nature." This formulation establishes a dual standard consisting of two components:
Objective Floor: The Receiving Party must exercise at least "reasonable care" — a well-understood legal standard that provides an objective, external benchmark for the adequacy of the Receiving Party's protective measures.
Subjective Benchmark: The Receiving Party must also exercise at least the same degree of care it applies to its own confidential information of a similar nature. This component ensures that a Receiving Party cannot treat the Disclosing Party's information with less care than it treats its own comparable information.
The dual standard is Velantis's required formulation. It is market-standard for M&A NDAs and provides the optimal balance of protection and practicality. The objective "reasonable care" floor ensures that even a counterparty with lax internal security practices cannot hide behind a low subjective standard; the subjective "own-information" benchmark ensures that a counterparty cannot treat Velantis's information as less important than its own.
7.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
The following modifications to the standard-of-care provision are acceptable without escalation:
•  Minor Wording Variations. Minor wording variations that preserve the substance of the dual standard are acceptable. For example, "at least the same degree of care" instead of "in no event less than the same degree of care" is substantively identical and may be accepted. Similarly, "reasonable measures" in place of "reasonable care" is acceptable, provided the "own-information" subjective benchmark is also retained.
•  Electronic-Information Specification. Addition of language specifying that the standard of care applies to electronic and digital information as well as physical information is acceptable. Such language may read, for example: "including by implementing and maintaining commercially reasonable administrative, technical, and physical safeguards to protect electronic Confidential Information." This specification is helpful for clarity and does not impose any obligation beyond what is already encompassed by the "reasonable care" standard.
7.3 — Requires Escalation
The following proposals regarding the standard of care require escalation:
•  Purely Subjective Standard. Proposals to adopt a purely subjective standard (e.g., "the same degree of care the Receiving Party uses to protect its own confidential information" or "the same degree of care the Receiving Party uses to protect its own confidential information of a similar nature") without the objective "reasonable care" floor require escalation. A purely subjective standard is inadequate if the counterparty's own internal practices are deficient — for example, if the counterparty does not maintain robust information-security protocols or routinely shares sensitive information with a broad group of employees. In such cases, the "own-information" standard would effectively require nothing, and Velantis's Confidential Information would be unprotected. While a purely subjective standard is not a Hard-Line rejection, it should generally be resisted, and the assigned attorney should escalate to Rachel Sung for a risk assessment before accepting.
7.4 — Hard Line
•  Heightened Care Standards. Velantis will not accept "highest degree of care," "utmost care," "extraordinary care," or any formulation that imposes a standard above reasonable care. Such standards are above market for M&A NDAs, expose Velantis to disproportionate liability, and are inconsistent with the degree of care that Velantis itself applies to its own confidential information. Accepting a heightened standard could create a situation in which Velantis is held to an aspirational (and potentially unattainable) standard of perfection in its handling of a counterparty's information — a standard that no reasonable organization can guarantee.
Counterparty proposals for heightened care standards must be rejected, and the assigned attorney should explain that the dual standard in Section 7.1 (objective "reasonable care" floor plus subjective "own-information" benchmark) is the market-standard formulation and provides robust protections for both parties. If the counterparty persists, the matter should be escalated to the General Counsel.
Section 8 — Compelled Disclosure and Protective Orders
8.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form recognizes that the Receiving Party may be compelled to disclose Confidential Information by law, regulation, governmental order, subpoena, civil investigative demand, or other legal process. The v.6.2 form permits such compelled disclosure, subject to the following conditions:
(i) Prior Notice. The Receiving Party shall provide prompt written notice to the Disclosing Party of any compelled disclosure obligation (to the extent legally permitted), including a description of the information to be disclosed and the applicable legal basis for the compulsion.
(ii) Cooperation. The Receiving Party shall use commercially reasonable efforts to cooperate with the Disclosing Party in seeking a protective order, confidential treatment request, or other appropriate remedy to minimize the scope of the compelled disclosure and to preserve the confidential nature of the disclosed information.
(iii) Minimum Disclosure. The Receiving Party shall disclose only that portion of the Confidential Information that is legally required to be disclosed, as advised by the Receiving Party's counsel.
(iv) Costs. Each party shall bear its own costs and expenses in connection with seeking any protective order, challenging any compelled disclosure, or otherwise responding to the circumstances giving rise to the compelled disclosure obligation.
This formulation balances the Receiving Party's legal obligations with the Disclosing Party's legitimate interest in protecting its Confidential Information. The "commercially reasonable efforts" standard for cooperation is appropriate because it does not require the Receiving Party to undertake extraordinary or economically disproportionate measures, while still imposing a meaningful obligation to assist the Disclosing Party.
8.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
The following modifications to the compelled-disclosure provision are acceptable without escalation:
•  Prior Written Consent. Proposals to add a requirement that the Receiving Party seek the Disclosing Party's prior written consent before making a compelled disclosure (in addition to the notice requirement) are acceptable, provided such consent may not be unreasonably withheld, conditioned, or delayed. This additional step provides the Disclosing Party with an opportunity to evaluate the compelled disclosure and potentially intervene before information is released, without creating a de facto veto that could expose the Receiving Party to contempt or other sanctions for failure to comply with a legal obligation.
•  Specified Notice Periods. Proposals to specify that notices under this section must be given within a stated number of hours or business days (e.g., within forty-eight (48) hours of receipt of the compulsion, or within two (2) business days) are acceptable provided the timeframe is reasonable in light of the circumstances. The assigned attorney should consider whether the proposed timeframe is practicable given the typical pace of legal process — for example, a forty-eight (48)-hour notice period may be unreasonable in the context of an emergency government investigation with a same-day production deadline. If the proposed timeframe appears too short to be reliably achievable, the assigned attorney should negotiate a longer period or add qualifying language (e.g., "to the extent practicable").
8.3 — Requires Escalation
•  No specific escalation items are identified for this section beyond the cost-shifting issue addressed as a Hard-Line item in Section 8.4 below. If a counterparty proposes unusual or novel modifications to the compelled-disclosure framework that are not addressed in Sections 8.1 or 8.2, the assigned attorney should use professional judgment and, if in doubt, escalate to Rachel Sung.
8.4 — Hard Line
•  Cost-Shifting. Cost-shifting provisions that require the Disclosing Party to bear all costs of seeking a protective order (or that require Velantis, in its capacity as Receiving Party, to bear all costs of seeking a protective order for the Disclosing Party's benefit) are rejected. Velantis's position is that each party bears its own costs and expenses in connection with any protective-order motion, challenge to compelled disclosure, or related proceeding, and that the Receiving Party's obligation is limited to cooperating with the Disclosing Party's efforts using commercially reasonable efforts.
Rationale: As a potential acquiror, Velantis is typically the Receiving Party in M&A NDAs. Accepting a cost-shifting provision that places all protective-order costs on the Receiving Party would effectively require Velantis to finance the Disclosing Party's litigation in response to third-party subpoenas, governmental requests, or other legal process — costs over which Velantis has no control and from which Velantis derives no benefit. Moreover, cost-shifting creates a moral hazard: if the Disclosing Party knows that the Receiving Party will bear all costs, the Disclosing Party has reduced incentive to prioritize or triage protective-order motions, potentially leading to unnecessary or disproportionately expensive litigation. The "each party bears its own costs" formulation is fair, balanced, and market-standard.
Section 9 — Return and Destruction of Materials
9.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form requires the Receiving Party to return or destroy all Confidential Information (including all copies, extracts, summaries, and Derivative Materials) upon the earlier of (i) termination of discussions regarding the Permitted Purpose or (ii) receipt of a written request by the Disclosing Party. The return or destruction must be completed within ten (10) business days following the applicable triggering event, and the Receiving Party must certify such return or destruction in writing. The certification must be signed by an authorized officer of the Receiving Party and must confirm that all Confidential Information (subject to the document-retention carve-out described in Section 9.3) has been returned or destroyed in accordance with the agreement.
The ten (10) business-day period is designed to provide the Receiving Party with sufficient time to identify, locate, and return or destroy all Confidential Information across its organization, including information held by external advisors. The authorized-officer certification requirement ensures accountability and creates a clear record of compliance.
9.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
The following modifications to the return/destruction provision are acceptable without escalation:
•  Shorter Return/Destruction Windows. A counterparty may propose shortening the return/destruction window. Windows of seven (7) to ten (10) business days are acceptable without escalation. A seven (7) business-day window, while tighter than the standard ten (10) business-day period, is achievable with appropriate internal coordination and advance planning.
•  Outside Counsel Certification. A counterparty may request that destruction be confirmed by a certificate signed by outside counsel rather than an authorized officer of the Receiving Party. This is acceptable. In some cases, outside counsel certification may be preferable because it provides an additional layer of professional responsibility — outside counsel has an ethical obligation to ensure the accuracy of the certification. The assigned attorney should confirm that the outside counsel certification covers the same scope as the authorized-officer certification (i.e., that all Confidential Information has been returned or destroyed, subject to the document-retention carve-out).
9.3 — Document-Retention Carve-Out
The v.6.2 form includes a bona fide document-retention carve-out, and this carve-out must be preserved in every NDA. The carve-out is essential to Velantis's operations and information-technology infrastructure and is not subject to deletion or material narrowing without escalation.
The document-retention carve-out provides that the Receiving Party is not required to return or destroy Confidential Information in the following circumstances:
(i) Electronic Backup and Archival Systems. Confidential Information retained in electronic backup, disaster-recovery, or archival systems made in the ordinary course of the Receiving Party's business need not be returned or destroyed, provided that the Receiving Party does not intentionally access such information after the return/destruction date. This exception recognizes the practical reality that modern enterprise IT environments employ automated backup systems — including tape backups, cloud-based disaster-recovery services, and email-archival platforms — that capture and retain data systematically, often without human intervention. Requiring a Receiving Party to purge individual files from these systems would be technically burdensome, prohibitively expensive, and in some cases, impossible without disrupting the integrity of the backup infrastructure.
(ii) Legal and Regulatory Retention Requirements. Confidential Information retained as required by applicable law, regulation, or bona fide internal document-retention policies need not be returned or destroyed. This exception recognizes that the Receiving Party may be subject to legal or regulatory requirements (e.g., SEC recordkeeping rules, IRS retention requirements, litigation-hold obligations) that mandate the retention of certain categories of documents, and that the NDA should not create a conflict between the Receiving Party's obligations under the NDA and its legal obligations.
Critically, all information retained under either prong of the document-retention carve-out remains subject to the confidentiality obligations of the agreement for the full duration of the confidentiality term. The carve-out permits retention, not use or disclosure.
9.4 — Requires Escalation
The following proposals regarding return and destruction of materials require escalation:
•  Deletion or Material Narrowing of Document-Retention Carve-Out. Proposals to delete or materially narrow the document-retention carve-out require escalation to Rachel Sung. The document-retention carve-out is a critical protection for Velantis, and its elimination would impose impractical and potentially impossible compliance obligations on Velantis's IT infrastructure. However, because a counterparty may have legitimate concerns about the scope of the carve-out (e.g., concerns that a Receiving Party could abuse the carve-out to retain large volumes of Confidential Information indefinitely), such proposals are classified as requiring escalation rather than as Hard-Line items, to permit a case-by-case assessment.
•  Return/Destruction Windows Shorter Than Seven Business Days. Proposals for return/destruction windows shorter than seven (7) business days require escalation. Windows of five (5) or six (6) business days may be achievable in some circumstances, but the assigned attorney should not agree to such windows without confirming feasibility with Velantis's IT team and outside advisors.
9.5 — Hard Line
•  Minimum Five-Business-Day Window. Velantis will not agree to a return/destruction window shorter than five (5) business days under any circumstances. A window of fewer than five business days does not provide sufficient time for Velantis to identify, collect, and return or destroy all Confidential Information across its organization, particularly where Confidential Information has been shared with outside advisors, financial institutions, and other third-party Representatives. If a counterparty insists on five (5) business days, this may be accepted only with escalation to Rachel Sung and only if the document-retention carve-out is preserved in full.
•  Omission of Document-Retention Carve-Out. Velantis will not agree to any NDA that omits the document-retention carve-out entirely. An NDA without a document-retention carve-out would require Velantis to purge Confidential Information from all electronic backup, disaster-recovery, and archival systems — an obligation that is technically infeasible and that could compromise the integrity of Velantis's information-technology infrastructure. If a counterparty insists on omitting the carve-out, the matter must be escalated to the General Counsel.
Section 10 — Non-Solicitation of Employees
10.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form includes a mutual non-solicitation provision. The non-solicitation provision prohibits each party from, for a period of eighteen (18) months following the date of the agreement, directly or indirectly soliciting for employment (or hiring, engaging, or retaining as an independent contractor or consultant) any employee of the other party with whom the soliciting party had contact or about whom the soliciting party received Confidential Information in connection with the Permitted Purpose.
The non-solicitation provision includes the following standard carve-outs:
(i) General Solicitations. The non-solicitation does not apply to general solicitations of employment not specifically targeted at the other party's employees, including job postings on the soliciting party's website, advertisements in newspapers or trade publications, postings on third-party job boards (e.g., LinkedIn, Indeed), and engagements with recruiting firms that are not specifically directed to recruit the other party's employees.
(ii) Unsolicited Inquiries. The non-solicitation does not apply to the hiring of any employee who contacts the soliciting party on his or her own initiative, without any direct or indirect solicitation by or encouragement from the soliciting party.
The non-solicitation provision is an important protection in the M&A context because the diligence process often involves extensive interaction between the parties' employees, including employees at the management and technical levels who possess specialized knowledge and industry relationships. Without a non-solicitation provision, a party could use the diligence process as a talent-recruitment exercise — identifying and targeting the other party's most valuable employees while ostensibly evaluating a potential transaction.
10.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
The following modifications to the non-solicitation provision are acceptable without escalation:
•  Shorter Non-Solicitation Periods. Non-solicitation periods equal to or shorter than eighteen (18) months are acceptable without escalation. For example, a twelve (12)-month non-solicitation period is commonly proposed by counterparties and is within the range of market norms for M&A NDAs. The assigned attorney may accept any period up to and including eighteen (18) months without seeking further internal approval.
•  Seniority Threshold. Proposals to limit the scope of the non-solicitation to employees above a specified seniority level (e.g., director-level and above, vice-president-level and above) are acceptable. Such limitations reduce the administrative burden of the non-solicitation provision and focus the restriction on the employees most likely to be the subject of targeted recruitment efforts during the diligence process.
•  Terminated-Employee Carve-Out. Proposals to add a carve-out for employees who are terminated by the Disclosing Party (i.e., the party whose employees are subject to the non-solicitation restriction) are acceptable. This carve-out recognizes that a terminated employee is no longer employed by the Disclosing Party and that the Disclosing Party has no continuing interest in restricting the employee's subsequent employment opportunities. The carve-out should apply only to employees terminated by the Disclosing Party and should not extend to employees who voluntarily resign.
10.3 — Requires Escalation
The following proposals regarding the non-solicitation provision require escalation:
•  Extension Beyond Eighteen Months. Proposals to extend the non-solicitation period beyond eighteen (18) months (e.g., to twenty-four (24) months or thirty-six (36) months) require escalation to Rachel Sung. The maximum period Velantis will accept after escalation is eighteen (18) months; there is no flexibility on this point. Non-solicitation periods longer than eighteen months are above market for M&A NDAs, may raise enforceability concerns in certain jurisdictions, and impose disproportionate restrictions on Velantis's ability to recruit talent in competitive markets. The assigned attorney should explain to the counterparty that eighteen months is the maximum term Velantis will accept and should not suggest that a longer term might be achievable through escalation.
10.4 — Remedies for Breach of Non-Solicitation
10.4(a) — Preferred Position.  The v.6.2 form provides that, in the event of a breach or threatened breach of the non-solicitation provision, the non-breaching party shall be entitled to seek equitable relief, including injunctive relief (temporary, preliminary, and permanent) and specific performance, in any court of competent jurisdiction, in addition to any other remedies available at law or in equity. The NDA further provides that each party acknowledges that a breach of the non-solicitation provision would cause irreparable harm for which monetary damages would be an inadequate remedy. This acknowledgment is important because it supports a motion for injunctive relief by establishing the "irreparable harm" element that most jurisdictions require for preliminary injunctive relief.
10.4(b) — Hard Line — Liquidated Damages.  
•  Velantis will not accept liquidated-damages provisions tied to non-solicitation breaches. Counterparty proposals for per-breach liquidated damages (e.g., "$150,000 per solicited employee," "$200,000 per hire in breach of the non-solicitation") are rejected. Liquidated damages are rejected for two reasons. First, they effectively cap Velantis's liability for non-solicitation breaches at a predetermined amount, which may be substantially less than the actual harm caused by the loss of a key employee — particularly a senior engineer, data scientist, or executive whose departure could disrupt an ongoing project or reveal sensitive strategic information to a competitor. Second, and more fundamentally, liquidated-damages provisions create a "price" for soliciting employees, transforming a covenant designed to deter misconduct into a cost-of-doing-business calculation. A well-capitalized counterparty could rationally conclude that the liquidated-damages payment is worth the benefit of recruiting a high-value employee, thereby undermining the deterrent purpose of the non-solicitation provision.
Equitable relief — injunctive relief and specific performance — is the preferred and required remedy for non-solicitation breaches. These remedies are designed to prevent the harm before it occurs (or mitigate ongoing harm), rather than compensating for it after the fact.
10.4(c) — Hard Line — Bond Requirement.  
•  Velantis will not accept any requirement that a party post a bond or other security (e.g., a surety bond, cash deposit, or letter of credit) as a precondition to seeking injunctive relief for a breach or threatened breach of the non-solicitation provision. The bond amounts typically proposed by counterparties (e.g., $500,000, $1,000,000) are arbitrary and serve no purpose other than to create a financial barrier to enforcement. Injunctive relief must be available without the posting of any bond or other security.
This position is consistent with Delaware Court of Chancery practice. The Delaware Court of Chancery does not customarily require the posting of a bond as a condition of granting preliminary injunctive relief in commercial disputes, and many M&A NDA disputes are adjudicated in the Court of Chancery. Imposing a contractual bond requirement would be inconsistent with Velantis's expectation that disputes under the NDA will be resolved in the Court of Chancery and could create confusion about the applicable procedural requirements.
Section 11 — Standstill
11.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form does not contain a standstill provision. It is Velantis's strong preference to exclude standstill provisions from NDAs in buy-side-initiated transactions entirely. This preference is reflected in the v.6.2 form, which is silent on standstill obligations.
Rationale: A standstill provision restricts the Receiving Party's ability to acquire the Disclosing Party's securities, make unsolicited acquisition proposals, or take other actions designed to effect a change of control of the Disclosing Party. Where Velantis has initiated the process as a potential acquiror — typically at the invitation of the counterparty's board of directors or management team — a standstill restricting Velantis's ability to make proposals or acquire securities is fundamentally incompatible with the purpose of the engagement. Velantis's access to Confidential Information in such situations is being granted for the specific purpose of enabling Velantis to evaluate and potentially make an acquisition proposal. A standstill would restrict the very activity that the NDA is designed to facilitate.
Standstill provisions are designed to protect targets in specific contexts — most commonly, unsolicited approaches, broadly marketed sell-side auction processes, and situations in which the target's board of directors seeks to maintain control over the timing and manner of a potential sale. In a consensual, buy-side-initiated context, these protections are unnecessary and inappropriate.
The assigned attorney should omit any standstill provision from the initial draft and should resist counterparty proposals to add one. If a counterparty proposes a standstill, the assigned attorney should not engage substantively with the proposal but should instead escalate immediately.
11.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
•  None. Any standstill proposal, regardless of its terms, requires escalation to Rachel Sung or the General Counsel. There are no standstill terms that the assigned attorney may accept independently.
11.3 — Requires Escalation (Maximum Concession)
If a counterparty insists on a standstill and the deal team determines that the relationship warrants a concession — for example, where the counterparty is a high-priority acquisition target and the standstill is the final obstacle to execution of the NDA — the following is the maximum concession Velantis will offer, subject to approval by Rachel Sung or the General Counsel:
•  Duration: The standstill must not exceed six (6) months from the date of the agreement. A six-month standstill is the maximum period Velantis will accept under any circumstances. The assigned attorney should open negotiations at three (3) months and concede to six (6) months only if the counterparty is firm. The rationale for a short standstill period is that M&A diligence processes typically resolve within three to six months, and a standstill that extends significantly beyond the expected diligence timeline serves no legitimate purpose.
•  Fall-Away Trigger: The standstill must contain an automatic fall-away trigger providing that the standstill terminates, without any action by either party, upon the public announcement or disclosure that a third party has made a bona fide proposal, offer, or indication of interest to acquire the counterparty or any material portion of its assets or equity (whether by merger, tender offer, exchange offer, asset purchase, or otherwise). The fall-away trigger is essential because it ensures that Velantis is not constrained from making a competing proposal if a third party enters the process. Without a fall-away trigger, Velantis could find itself sidelined while a competitor negotiates a transaction with the counterparty — a commercially unacceptable outcome.
•  No "Don't-Ask-Don't-Waive" (DADW): The standstill must not include a "don't-ask-don't-waive" provision. A DADW provision prohibits the standstill-bound party from privately requesting that the counterparty's board of directors waive the standstill, and prohibits the counterparty's board from granting such a waiver without publicly disclosing the request. DADW provisions are fundamentally anticompetitive: they chill private communications between the parties, inhibit the counterparty's board from fulfilling its fiduciary duties (including its duty to consider bona fide acquisition proposals in the best interests of its stockholders), and have been the subject of significant criticism in the Delaware courts and the academic literature. Velantis must retain the right to privately request a waiver of the standstill from the counterparty's board of directors (or equivalent governing body) at any time, and the counterparty's board must remain free to grant such a waiver in its discretion.
11.4 — Hard Line
The following standstill-related positions are Hard-Line items that Velantis will not accept under any circumstances:
•  Duration Exceeding Six Months. Velantis will not accept a standstill with a duration exceeding six (6) months from the date of the agreement. Proposals for twelve (12)-month, eighteen (18)-month, or longer standstills are rejected without further discussion.
•  No Fall-Away Trigger. Velantis will not accept a standstill that does not contain a fall-away trigger upon a third-party bid. A standstill without a fall-away trigger could lock Velantis out of a competitive process, which is commercially unacceptable.
•  Don't-Ask-Don't-Waive Provisions. Velantis will not accept a "don't-ask-don't-waive" provision under any circumstances. DADW provisions are rejected regardless of the standstill duration, the scope of the standstill, or any other concessions the counterparty may offer in exchange. This position is firm and is not subject to negotiation or trade-off.
•  Summary Rejection. Proposals for twelve (12)-month standstills or standstills with DADW provisions are rejected without further discussion. The assigned attorney should promptly communicate Velantis's rejection to the counterparty and, if the counterparty persists, escalate to the General Counsel for a determination on whether to terminate discussions.
Section 12 — Governing Law
12.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form provides that the agreement shall be governed by and construed in accordance with the laws of the State of Delaware, without regard to conflict-of-laws principles that might otherwise require the application of the laws of another jurisdiction. The "without regard to conflict-of-laws principles" language is critical and must be retained in every NDA, as it prevents a court from applying a choice-of-law analysis that could result in the application of a different state's law despite the parties' contractual choice of Delaware.
12.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
•  None. Delaware governing law is required in every M&A NDA to which Velantis is a party. There are no alternative governing-law provisions that the assigned attorney may accept without escalation. This is a bright-line rule that admits no exceptions.
12.3 — Hard Line
•  Delaware Governing Law Required. Governing law must be Delaware. Proposals for any other governing law — including, without limitation, Virginia (where Velantis maintains certain operational facilities), New York (a common counterparty preference), California (frequently proposed by West Coast counterparties), or any foreign jurisdiction — are rejected.
Rationale: Velantis is a Delaware corporation, incorporated under the Delaware General Corporation Law. The Delaware Court of Chancery, which is Velantis's preferred forum for dispute resolution (see Section 13), has the most well-developed body of precedent in the country for M&A-related disputes, including disputes arising under non-disclosure agreements, standstill provisions, and related contractual arrangements. Velantis's v.6.2 form NDA is drafted against the backdrop of Delaware statutory and common law, and its provisions — including the equitable-relief provisions, the jury-trial waiver, and the standstill framework — are designed to be interpreted and enforced under Delaware law. Accepting another state's law would introduce interpretive uncertainty, could conflict with the dispute-resolution and jurisdiction provisions of the NDA (see Section 13), and would require a case-by-case analysis of whether each provision of the NDA is enforceable under the alternative governing law — an analysis that the Legal Department has determined is not justified by any legitimate commercial benefit.
Section 13 — Dispute Resolution and Jurisdiction
13.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form provides for the exclusive jurisdiction of the Delaware Court of Chancery (or, if the Court of Chancery declines jurisdiction, the United States District Court for the District of Delaware) for all disputes, controversies, claims, and causes of action arising out of or relating to the agreement. Each party irrevocably consents to the personal jurisdiction of the foregoing courts, irrevocably waives any objection to the laying of venue in such courts, and irrevocably waives any right to remove any action from such courts to any other court or tribunal.
In addition, each party irrevocably waives any right to a trial by jury in any action, proceeding, or counterclaim arising out of or relating to the agreement. The jury-trial waiver is a critical provision: it ensures that disputes under the NDA will be decided by a judge (in the Court of Chancery, by a Chancellor or Vice Chancellor sitting without a jury), which promotes predictability, consistency, and efficiency in the resolution of NDA disputes.
13.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
The following modifications to the dispute-resolution and jurisdiction provisions are acceptable without escalation:
•  Superior Court Fallback. Proposals to add the Superior Court of the State of Delaware as a fallback forum (after the Court of Chancery and the U.S. District Court for the District of Delaware) are acceptable. The Superior Court is a court of general jurisdiction in Delaware and is an appropriate alternative if neither the Court of Chancery nor the federal district court has jurisdiction over a particular claim. The assigned attorney should ensure that the Superior Court is listed as a third-priority forum (i.e., after the Court of Chancery and the U.S. District Court), not as the primary or co-equal forum.
•  General Consent to Personal Jurisdiction. Proposals to include a general consent to personal jurisdiction in the selected Delaware courts are acceptable, provided that exclusive venue remains in Delaware. Such provisions are often included as part of a comprehensive forum-selection clause and do not alter the substance of Velantis's preferred position.
13.3 — Hard Line
The following dispute-resolution positions are Hard-Line items:
•  No Binding Arbitration. Velantis will not accept binding arbitration — whether administered under JAMS, AAA (American Arbitration Association), ICC (International Chamber of Commerce), UNCITRAL, or any other arbitral rules or institution — as the dispute-resolution mechanism for an M&A NDA.
Rationale: Arbitration is inconsistent with Velantis's need for rapid injunctive relief in the event of a breach or threatened breach of the NDA. Injunctive relief is best obtained from the Delaware Court of Chancery, which has established procedures for expedited proceedings and temporary restraining orders in commercial disputes. While some arbitral rules provide for emergency arbitrator procedures, these procedures are generally less efficient, less predictable, and less well-developed than the Court of Chancery's expedited proceedings. Moreover, arbitration proceedings are confidential by default, which may limit Velantis's strategic flexibility — for example, by preventing Velantis from publicizing a counterparty's breach as part of a defensive strategy in a contested M&A situation. Finally, the limited appellate review available in arbitration (under the Federal Arbitration Act, judicial review of arbitral awards is extremely narrow) is inconsistent with the importance of the rights at stake in an M&A NDA dispute.
•  Delaware Forum Required. Velantis will not accept any forum other than Delaware for the resolution of disputes arising under an M&A NDA. Proposals for Virginia, New York, California, or any other domestic or foreign jurisdiction are rejected. The rationale for this position is set forth in Section 12.3 (governing law) and is incorporated here by reference. Consistency between the governing-law provision (Delaware) and the forum-selection provision (Delaware) is essential to avoid the procedural complexity and potential inconsistency that would arise if a court in one state were required to apply another state's law.
Section 14 — Equitable Relief and Bond
14.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form includes a robust equitable-relief provision that is applicable to breaches of any obligation under the NDA (not limited to the non-solicitation provision addressed in Section 10.4). The provision states that each party acknowledges that a breach of the agreement would cause irreparable harm to the Disclosing Party for which monetary damages would be an inadequate remedy, and that the non-breaching party shall be entitled to seek equitable relief, including injunctive relief (temporary, preliminary, and permanent) and specific performance, in any court of competent jurisdiction, without the necessity of proving actual damages and without the posting of any bond or other security.
This provision serves several important functions. First, the mutual acknowledgment of irreparable harm creates a contractual stipulation that supports a motion for preliminary injunctive relief by satisfying the "irreparable harm" element that most courts require. Second, the "without the necessity of proving actual damages" language eliminates a potential procedural hurdle — some courts have required movants for injunctive relief to demonstrate that they have suffered (or will suffer) quantifiable damages, and this language forecloses that argument. Third, the "without bond" language waives the bond requirement that would otherwise apply under Rule 65(c) of the Federal Rules of Civil Procedure or its state-law equivalents.
14.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
The following modifications to the equitable-relief provision are acceptable without escalation:
•  "In Addition to" Language. Proposals to add language stating that equitable relief is available "in addition to all other remedies available at law or in equity" are acceptable and, in fact, preferred. This language makes clear that the equitable-relief provision is cumulative — it does not supplant or limit any claim for monetary damages, indemnification, or other legal relief that may be available to the non-breaching party.
•  Express Damages Reservation. Proposals to add a clause providing that "the foregoing shall not limit the non-breaching party's right to pursue monetary damages for any breach of this agreement" are acceptable. This language serves the same function as the "in addition to" language described above and provides additional clarity.
14.3 — Hard Line
•  No Bond Requirement. Velantis will not accept any requirement that a party post a bond, surety, cash deposit, letter of credit, or other form of security as a precondition to obtaining injunctive relief for a breach or threatened breach of the NDA. Bond requirements — whether set at a fixed amount (e.g., $500,000, $1,000,000) or at a discretionary amount to be determined by the court — are rejected.
Rationale: The "without bond" formulation is standard for M&A NDAs and is consistent with Delaware Court of Chancery practice, where bonds are not customarily required as a condition of granting preliminary injunctions in commercial cases. A bond requirement would create an unnecessary financial barrier to enforcement. In a time-sensitive M&A context — where a breach of the NDA could cause immediate and irreversible competitive harm (e.g., disclosure of acquisition strategy, dissemination of proprietary financial models, solicitation of key employees) — any delay in obtaining injunctive relief, including a delay attributable to the logistics of posting a bond, could be devastating. Moreover, the bond amounts typically proposed by counterparties (e.g., $500,000) are arbitrary: they bear no relationship to the actual damages at stake and serve no purpose other than to deter enforcement.
Section 15 — No Obligation to Transact
15.1 — Preferred Position
The v.6.2 form includes a provision stating clearly and unambiguously:
"Nothing herein shall obligate either party to consummate any transaction, and either party may terminate discussions at any time for any reason or no reason."
This language serves a critical function: it makes clear that the NDA does not constitute an agreement in principle, a letter of intent, a binding commitment to negotiate, or any other form of binding obligation to proceed with the contemplated transaction. The NDA governs only the handling of Confidential Information; the decision to proceed (or not proceed) with a transaction remains entirely within each party's discretion.
The "no obligation to transact" provision is important for both legal and commercial reasons. Legally, it forecloses any claim that the NDA creates an implied or constructive obligation to negotiate in good faith or to consummate a deal. Commercially, it preserves Velantis's flexibility to walk away from a transaction at any time during the diligence process if the opportunity does not meet Velantis's strategic or financial criteria.
15.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
The following modifications to the "no obligation to transact" provision are acceptable without escalation:
•  No Rights in Confidential Information. Proposals to add language confirming that neither party acquires any rights in the other party's Confidential Information — other than the limited right to use such information for the Permitted Purpose as set forth in the NDA — are acceptable. Such language is implicit in the structure of the NDA (which grants a limited license to use Confidential Information for the Permitted Purpose and requires return or destruction thereafter) but making it explicit may provide useful clarity.
•  No Relationship Creation. Proposals to confirm that the NDA does not create a partnership, joint venture, agency relationship, employment relationship, or fiduciary relationship between the parties are acceptable. This is standard boilerplate and is consistent with the v.6.2 form's overall framework.
15.3 — Requires Escalation
•  "No Obligation to Negotiate in Good Faith" Language. Proposals to add language stating that "neither party shall have any obligation to continue discussions or negotiate in good faith" are flagged as problematic and require escalation to Rachel Sung.
While the underlying concept — that neither party is bound to negotiate or reach an agreement — is acceptable and is already captured by the "no obligation to transact" formulation, the explicit disclaimer of any obligation to "negotiate in good faith" raises a distinct concern. Such language may be read as sanctioning bad-faith conduct during the diligence process — for example, a party that uses diligence access to gain competitive intelligence (reviewing the counterparty's customer lists, pricing data, product roadmap, and key-employee compensation) with no genuine interest in consummating a transaction. While the NDA's use restrictions and confidentiality obligations provide some protection against such misuse, an express disclaimer of good-faith obligations could undermine those protections by providing the bad-faith party with a contractual defense to a claim of misuse.
Velantis's preferred approach is to use the cleaner formulation set forth in Section 15.1 — "Nothing herein shall obligate either party to consummate any transaction" — which achieves the same commercial objective (preserving deal flexibility) without the negative implication (condoning bad-faith engagement). If a counterparty insists on the "no obligation to negotiate in good faith" language, the assigned attorney should escalate to Rachel Sung for a determination on whether additional protective provisions (e.g., a strengthened non-use covenant, tighter restrictions on the scope of information disclosed, or enhanced monitoring requirements) are warranted to offset the risk.
15.4 — Hard Line
•  No specific Hard-Line items are identified for this provision. However, the assigned attorney should note that the protections against misuse of Confidential Information — including the Permitted Purpose restriction (Section 4), the residuals clause and its limitations (Section 6), and the general confidentiality and non-use obligations of the NDA — collectively serve as the primary safeguards against bad-faith engagement. Any erosion of those protections is governed by the Hard-Line positions set forth in the respective sections of this Playbook.

16.2 — Acceptable Without Escalation
The following modifications to the notices provision are acceptable without escalation:
•  Email-Only Notice. Proposals to permit notice by email only (without a parallel hard-copy requirement) are acceptable provided the email includes a read-receipt or delivery-confirmation mechanism, or the NDA provides that email notice is effective upon the sender's receipt of confirmation that the email was delivered to the recipient's inbox. The assigned attorney should ensure that the email notice provision includes a mechanism for confirming delivery, as email is inherently less reliable than physical delivery and may be subject to spam filters, server outages, or other technical failures.
•  Registered Mail. Proposals to add registered or certified mail (with return receipt requested) as a permitted notice method are acceptable. Registered mail provides a verifiable record of delivery and is a commonly used notice method in commercial agreements.
Section 17 — Miscellaneous Provisions
17.1 — Integration / Entire Agreement
The v.6.2 form includes a standard integration clause providing that the NDA constitutes the entire agreement between the parties with respect to the subject matter hereof and supersedes all prior and contemporaneous agreements, understandings, negotiations, and discussions, whether oral or written, between the parties relating to the subject matter hereof. No modifications to this clause are anticipated or acceptable beyond clarifying that prior oral discussions regarding confidentiality (e.g., preliminary conversations between business personnel in which the parties informally agreed to treat certain information as confidential) are superseded by the NDA. This clarification may be helpful where the parties engaged in substantive discussions before the NDA was executed and may have informal understandings about confidentiality that could create ambiguity.
17.2 — Amendment and Waiver
The v.6.2 form provides that no amendment, modification, or supplement to the agreement shall be valid unless made in writing and signed by both parties. The form further provides that no waiver by either party of any provision of the agreement shall be deemed a continuing waiver or a waiver of any other provision, and that the failure of either party to enforce any provision of the agreement at any time shall not constitute a waiver of such party's right to enforce such provision in the future. These provisions are standard and should not be modified. They protect Velantis from arguments that a course of conduct (e.g., repeated non-enforcement of a particular obligation) constitutes a waiver of that obligation.
17.3 — Assignment
The v.6.2 form prohibits assignment of the NDA without the prior written consent of the other party, except that either party may assign the NDA without consent (i) to an affiliate of the assigning party, or (ii) in connection with a merger, consolidation, reorganization, or sale of all or substantially all of the assets of the assigning party or the business unit to which the NDA relates. This language is standard and should be retained. The affiliate-assignment exception is necessary to accommodate intra-group reorganizations, and the change-of-control exception ensures that the NDA remains in effect (and enforceable) following a transformative corporate transaction.
17.4 — Severability
The v.6.2 form includes standard severability language providing that if any provision of the agreement is held to be invalid, illegal, or unenforceable, the remaining provisions shall continue in full force and effect, and the invalid provision shall be reformed to the minimum extent necessary to make it valid, legal, and enforceable while preserving the parties' original intent. This language should be retained without modification. The severability clause is particularly important in the context of non-solicitation provisions (Section 10) and standstill provisions (Section 11), which may be subject to enforceability challenges in certain jurisdictions.
Section 18 — Escalation Procedures
18.1 — Escalation Authority
All matters requiring escalation under this Playbook must be directed to Rachel Sung, VP & Associate General Counsel, Velantis Technologies, Inc. Rachel Sung has authority to approve, reject, or modify any counterparty position designated "Requires Escalation" in this Playbook, subject to the limitations set forth herein. In Rachel Sung's absence (e.g., due to leave, travel, or unavailability), escalation should be directed to the General Counsel.
For matters involving a Hard-Line position, the General Counsel is the ultimate decision-maker. Rachel Sung may recommend a course of action with respect to a Hard-Line item, but only the General Counsel (or, in extraordinary circumstances, the Chief Executive Officer acting on the General Counsel's recommendation) may authorize a deviation from a Hard-Line position.
18.2 — Escalation Format
Escalation requests should be submitted by email to Rachel Sung (or the General Counsel, as applicable) with the subject line formatted as follows:
NDA Escalation — [Project Code Name]
Example: NDA Escalation — Project Lantern
Each escalation request must include the following information:
(i) Playbook Provision. Identification of the specific Playbook section and subsection at issue (e.g., "Section 11.3 — Standstill — Requires Escalation").
(ii) Counterparty Language. The counterparty's proposed language, presented in a Track Changes redline against the v.6.2 form so that the deviation is clearly visible.
(iii) Attorney Assessment and Recommendation. The assigned attorney's assessment of the counterparty's proposal, including (a) an analysis of the legal risks associated with accepting the proposal, (b) a comparison to market practice (if relevant), and (c) a recommendation to accept, reject, or propose a counter-modification.
(iv) Commercial Context. Any relevant commercial context provided by the deal team, including the strategic importance of the transaction, the status of negotiations, the counterparty's rationale for the proposal (if known), and any trade-offs or concessions that the deal team has identified.
The escalation email should attach the most recent draft of the NDA (with Track Changes) and any relevant correspondence with the counterparty or the deal team.
18.3 — Response Time
Rachel Sung or the General Counsel will respond to escalation requests within one (1) business day during active negotiations. The Legal Department recognizes that NDA negotiations in the M&A context are often time-sensitive and that delays in responding to counterparty proposals can jeopardize the deal relationship. Accordingly, every effort will be made to provide timely responses to escalation requests.
For matters involving Hard-Line positions — where the counterparty is insisting on a position that Velantis has determined is non-negotiable — a same-day response will be provided during business hours (defined as 8:00 a.m. to 7:00 p.m. Eastern Time, Monday through Friday). The same-day response commitment reflects the urgency of Hard-Line situations, which may require a rapid determination on whether to terminate discussions or authorize an extraordinary deviation.
If Rachel Sung or the General Counsel requires additional information before responding to an escalation request, the assigned attorney will be notified promptly and should provide the requested information on an expedited basis.
